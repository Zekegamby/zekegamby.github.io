The research · AI to physical hardware

The process
behind the prototype.

A more direct path from AI-generated design to a working mechanism.

My work connects AI-assisted design with the geometry and material instructions used to print a physical object. I’m adapting the design-to-print workflow so a robot’s outer shape, internal mechanisms and hard/soft regions can be developed together.

That makes the body itself part of what can change between iterations: the joints, the tendon paths, the structure and where different materials belong.

The modified workflow

Design decisions become physical structure.

  1. 01 / Design

    Generate form and material.

    Use AI to help develop the geometry, internal features and distribution of rigid and flexible materials.

  2. 02 / Translate & print

    Make the design printable.

    Bring those geometry and material decisions into print-ready instructions for the PolyJet system.

  3. 03 / Test & revise

    Learn from the real mechanism.

    Check the printed result, identify what limits movement, and use those observations to guide another version.

Current development is directed and tested by me. Automating more of this feedback loop is the research direction.

Why this matters.

Designing around bought components means working with their existing shapes and capabilities. Separate fabrication and assembly steps can also make a promising design difficult to build or revise.

I’m exploring how much of a robot’s function can be designed into its printed body. The aim is to give AI more freedom to change the mechanism itself, while reducing the work between a design change and a physical test.

What could change
in one iteration?

A finger that struggles to bend might need a different joint, a better tendon path or a change in its flexible regions. This workflow lets me investigate those choices together in the next printed design.

The question is whether those changes improve the physical result. That is what the prototypes are for.

What is modified—and what comes from PolyJet?

PolyJet already provides multimaterial printing and voxel-level material control. My work builds on that platform by developing the design-to-print workflow around AI-generated geometry and material instructions, and testing it through robotic mechanisms.

The contribution I’m investigating is how that connection supports the creation and revision of functional hardware. The public description covers what the workflow enables; it leaves out the implementation details.

What I’ve built so far

I’ve progressed from individual printed fingers to the thumb-and-index prototype shown above. Current tests focus on joint movement, tendon routing and tension. Motors and electronics remain external to the printed structure.

The longer-term goal is a repeated cycle of simulation, printing, physical testing and redesign, with AI improving both the body and how it moves.
